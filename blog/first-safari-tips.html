Travel Tips

First Safari? 15 Essential Tips Before You Go

Reyia SaitotiFounder & Lead Guide
📅 June 2025 ⏱ 8 min read

A first safari is one of the most anticipated experiences of a lifetime — and one of the most misunderstood. I have guided hundreds of first-timers into the Maasai Mara, and I see the same questions, the same mistakes, and the same moments of pure awe every time. This guide is what I wish every guest could read before they arrive.

"The bush does not perform on command. It rewards the patient, the quiet, and the ones who stop looking at their phone long enough to feel where they actually are."

Before You Leave Home

1

Book more nights than you think you need

The most common regret I hear from departing guests is: "I wish we had stayed longer." Three nights is the minimum for a meaningful Mara experience. Five nights gives you the full rhythm of the bush — the early mornings, the midday surprises, the golden-hour drives. Book at least one more night than feels necessary.

2

Get travel insurance that covers emergency evacuation

Medical facilities in remote safari areas are limited. A serious injury requires emergency air evacuation — costs can exceed USD 50,000 without insurance. This is non-negotiable. AMREF Flying Doctors is widely recommended for Kenya visitors.

3

Sort your malaria prevention before you travel

The Maasai Mara is in a malaria zone. Speak to a travel doctor about prophylactics at least 2 weeks before departure — options include Malarone, doxycycline, and mefloquine. Also bring a good DEET-based repellent and sleep under a net.

4

Soft-sided luggage only for bush flights

If you are taking a small bush aircraft (common for safari transfers), hard-shell suitcases cannot fit in the baggage holds. Use a soft duffel bag. Maximum luggage allowance on most small aircraft is 15kg — pack accordingly.

What to Pack

🎽 Clothing

  • Khaki, olive, brown, beige layers
  • Long-sleeved shirts (sun & insects)
  • Light fleece or jacket (cold mornings)
  • Comfortable trousers — not jeans
  • Wide-brim hat
  • Comfortable closed shoes
  • Sandals for lodge evenings

📷 Gear & Essentials

  • Binoculars — 8×42 minimum
  • Camera with zoom lens (200mm+)
  • Extra memory cards and batteries
  • Power bank — charging limited
  • Universal travel adapter
  • Headlamp/torch
  • Dry bag or dustproof camera bag

💊 Health & Comfort

  • Malaria prophylactics
  • High-SPF sunscreen (50+)
  • DEET insect repellent
  • Rehydration sachets
  • Personal prescription medication
  • Antihistamines
  • Lip balm and moisturiser

🚫 Leave at Home

  • Bright white or blue clothing
  • Perfume or strong cologne
  • Noisy jewellery or accessories
  • Hard-shell suitcases (bush flights)
  • Plastic bags (banned in Kenya)
  • Drones without special permits

Early morning game drives — starting before 6:30am — are always the most rewarding

In the Bush — How to Behave

5

Wake up early — every single morning

I cannot stress this enough. The first two hours after sunrise are when predators are active, when the light is magical, and when the bush is at its most alive. Every morning you sleep through is an experience you cannot recover. Set your alarm for 5:45am and embrace it.

6

Stay in the vehicle at all times unless your guide says otherwise

Animals perceive the vehicle as a large, neutral object. The moment you stand up or step out, you become a recognisable human shape — and animal behaviour changes immediately. Your guide will tell you when it is safe to exit. Trust them.

7

Be quiet — especially near predators

Loud voices, sudden movements, and mobile phone ringtones disturb animals and ruin sightings for everyone in the vehicle. Whisper. Move slowly. Turn your phone to silent before every drive.

8

Wear muted colours on every drive

Wildlife has excellent colour vision. Bright colours — especially white and blue — stand out in the bush and make animals nervous. Khaki, olive, brown, and grey blend into the landscape. This is not a fashion choice, it is a wildlife-viewing strategy.

9

Learn to use binoculars before you arrive

An astonishing number of guests arrive without ever having properly used binoculars and cannot focus them quickly enough when an animal appears. Practice at home — focusing quickly on a moving bird in your garden is exactly the skill you will need in the Mara.

10

Put the camera down sometimes

I watch guests spend an entire lion sighting looking at the animal through a phone screen rather than with their own eyes. Take your photos — then put the camera down, breathe, and simply be there. The experience matters more than the image.

Photography Tips for First-Timers

11

Shoot in burst mode for moving animals

A running cheetah or leaping impala is over in two seconds. Burst mode captures every frame — you can delete the blurry ones later. On a phone, hold the shutter button down.

12

Shoot at the animal's eye level whenever possible

Ask your driver to lower the vehicle side or position so you can shoot horizontally at the animal. A lion photographed at eye level is a completely different — and far more powerful — image than one shot from above.

13

Use the golden hours — dawn and dusk only

Midday African light is harsh and flat. The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset give you warm, directional light that turns every image golden. If you can only do one drive per day, make it the morning drive.

Mindset and Expectations

14

The bush does not guarantee sightings

Wildlife is wild. Some days you find a leopard with cubs in the first ten minutes. Other days you drive for hours and see only zebra and impala. Both days are real safari. The unpredictability is part of what makes it extraordinary — you cannot stream this, you cannot replay it. It either happens or it doesn't, and that is the point.

15

Trust your guide completely

A good local guide is not just a driver — they are reading tracks, listening to birdsong, watching animal body language, and processing information you cannot see. When your guide says "there is a lion in that thicket," they are not guessing. Give them your trust and they will give you the wild.

Kenya Safari Quick Reference

  • Currency: Kenyan Shilling (KES) — USD widely accepted in parks
  • Tipping: USD 10–20 per guide per day is standard and appreciated
  • Electricity: 240V, UK-style 3-pin plugs — bring a universal adapter
  • Water: Drink bottled or lodge-provided filtered water only
  • Photography: Ask before photographing Maasai people — a small tip is appropriate
  • Altitude: Nairobi is at 1,700m — allow a day to acclimatise before heading to the bush
